How to learn multiplication tables fast?
5 proven steps + free practice

📅 Nov 17, 2026 ✍️ EduPlay Team ⏱️ 9 min read
🤔 Two kids learn the times table together. Kid A recites from "one times one is one" all the way to "nine times nine" perfectly — but freezes at 56÷7, because he only learned to recite forward, never backward. Kid B spends one day understanding that multiplication is just "groups of", then uses patterns and games. Three weeks later, both 8×7 and 56÷7 are instant.
The difference is not memory. It is method.

This article covers three things: why times tables are hard to memorize, 5 techniques that actually work, and how to go from "can recite" to "can use" in 3 weeks — plus a totally free mental-math game you can use today.

Why are multiplication tables so hard to memorize?

Do not blame the kid first. A times table has 81 cells. Treat them as 81 unrelated chants to cram and working memory simply overflows — forgetting the later rows while learning them is completely normal.

Second, rote chants are sounds, not meaning. A child can say "seven times eight is fifty-six" without knowing how it relates to seven 8s added together. Shuffle the order or use it in division, and the chain breaks.

Third, memorizing without practicing is not memorizing. A fact only moves from "I can recall it" to "it comes out instantly" after repeated timed use.

Step 1: Understand what multiplication means

Before memorizing, spend 10 minutes making one idea click — it is the foundation for everything below:

① Multiplication is "groups of"

3×4 means three 4s added together (4+4+4), and also four 3s. Use something familiar: "3 bags of candy, 4 in each bag — how many?" Let them lay it out and count.

② The commutative rule halves the work

3×4 and 4×3 give the same answer. Tell your child "there are only 45 facts to learn, not 81" — the mental load drops in half and they are far more willing to start.

5 memory techniques (far better than rote drilling)

Use these in order — most kids master the whole table within 3 weeks:

① Learn 2, 5 and 9 first

The 2s are just doubles (2, 4, 6, 8…); the 5s end only in 0 or 5; the 9s count up in tens and down in units (09, 18, 27…). These three groups have the strongest patterns — winning them first gives kids a taste of success.

② Pin the hard facts with stories

The toughest is usually something like 7×8=56. Make an image: "7×8=56 — on July 8th I ate 56 candies." The stranger and more visual the association, the longer it sticks.

③ Recite forward, then ask backward

Reciting "seven times eight is fifty-six" is not enough. Randomly ask "56 is what times what?" or "8 times what is 56?" — backward questions train division at the same time.

④ 10 minutes a day, little and often

Rather than a 2-hour weekend cram, do 10 minutes a day in two short sessions. A one-minute review of the day's new facts just before bed lets sleep consolidate them.

⑤ Use a game to make it useful

It only counts once recall is timed. A mental-math race game generates random questions with instant feedback — far more fun (and effective) than copying a fact ten times.

A 3-week path: from reciting to using

Split three weeks into clear stages with one goal each, so kids rarely feel defeated:

Week 1: just get the recitation smooth

Get the 2s, 5s and 9s flowing, then add 3, 4 and 6. Do not chase speed this week — smooth and unstuck is enough.

Week 2: practice out of order

Start random questions and backward questions. Write the facts they keep missing on cards for the wall and attack the weak spots instead of practicing everything evenly.

Week 3: move it into math and division

Use timed mental math to turn facts into instant, automatic answers, and add division like 56÷7. Only when they can use facts inside problems have they truly learned them.

FAQ

What grade should kids start learning times tables?

Usually grade 2. Most curricula introduce the meaning of multiplication in grade 2 and expect fluent facts soon after. Understanding first, then memorizing, beats forcing it in grade 1.

They know it in order but not shuffled — what now?

That is classic sequence memory, not real learning. The fix is random questions plus timed mental math: break the order so the brain must fetch the answer directly. A week or two usually fixes it.

Do they still need mental-math practice after memorizing?

Yes. Facts are the raw material; mental math is the real game. Only repeated timed use in random questions turns "let me think" into "instant" — exactly the stage-based method in our mental-math guide.
